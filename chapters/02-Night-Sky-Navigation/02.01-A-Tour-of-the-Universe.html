Earth's Address

Let's take a brief tour of the universe to get acquainted with the types of objects and distances we will encounter throughout this textbook. During this tour, we will be using a lot of new terms. At this point, you do not need to be familiar with every word in this section. The purpose of this tour is to give you an idea of the scale of space and the types of objects we observe. With that in mind, let's get started.

Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\) is an image of our home, Earth, a nearly spherical planet about 13,000 kilometers in diameter. A space traveler entering our planetary system would easily distinguish Earth from the other planets in our Solar System by the large amount of liquid water that covers roughly two thirds of its surface. If the traveler had equipment to receive radio or television signals, or came close enough to see the lights of our cities at night, they would soon find signs that this watery planet has intelligent life.

Our nearest astronomical neighbor is Earth's natural satellite, commonly called the Moon. Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\) is images of Earth and the Moon with their relative sizes and distance to scale. Notice how small we have to make these objects to fit them in the same figure. The Moon's distance from Earth is about 30 times Earth's diameter, or approximately 384,000 kilometers/ It takes about a month for the Moon to orbit around Earth. The Moon's diameter is 3476 kilometers, about one fourth the size of Earth.

Light takes 1.3 seconds to travel between Earth and the Moon. If you've seen videos of missions to the Moon, you may recall that there was a delay of about 3 seconds during conversations. This was not because it took the radio waves almost 3 seconds to make the round trip. Note that radio waves are a type of light.

Earth orbits around our star, the Sun, which is about 150 million kilometers away, which is approximately 400 times as far away from us as the Moon. We call the average Earth–Sun distance an astronomical unit (AU). The astronomical unit is convenient because it is easier than writing out the distance in kilometers, and when we use the unit for measuring the distance of other planets, we can easily scale it to our distance from the Sun. Light takes slightly more than 8 minutes to travel 1 astronomical unit, which means the latest news we receive from the Sun is always 8 minutes old. The diameter of the Sun is about 1.5 million kilometers. Earth could fit comfortably inside one of the minor eruptions that occurs on the surface of our star. As an example of scale, if the Sun were the size of a basketball, Earth would be a small apple seed about 30 meters from the ball.

It takes Earth 1 year to go around the Sun at our distance, which means we are travelling at a speed of roughly 110,000 kilometers per hour. Because gravity holds us firmly to Earth and there is no resistance to Earth's motion in the vacuum of space, we participate in this extremely fast-moving trip without being aware of it day to day.

Earth is only one of eight planets that orbit around the Sun. Figure \(\PageIndex{3}\) These planets, along with their moons and swarms of smaller bodies such as dwarf planets, make up the Solar System. A planet is a roughly spherical object, that orbits a star in a path that is cleared of smaller objects. This definition rules out other objects, such as comets and asteroids, that orbit the Sun, but are either not spherical or live in a belt with many objects that have similar orbits.

We are able to see the nearby planets in our skies only because they reflect the light of the Sun. If the planets were much farther away, the tiny amount of light they reflect would usually not be visible to us. The exoplanets we have discovered orbiting other stars were found from the pull their gravity exerts on their stars, or from the light they block from their stars when they pass in front of them. It is very difficult to observe them directly because they are very faint and right next a bright star, but astronomers have managed to collect images of a few exoplanets.

Like our Sun, all the other stars are enormous balls of glowing gas that generate vast amounts of energy by nuclear reactions in their core. The other stars look faint only because they very far away. If we continue our basketball analogy, Proxima Centauri, the nearest star beyond the Sun, which is 4.3 light-years away, would be almost 7000 kilometers from the basketball.

When you look up at a star-filled sky on a clear night, all the stars visible to the unaided eye are part of a single collection of stars we call the Milky Way Galaxy. The Sun is one of hundreds of billions of stars that make up the Milky Way. Within a sphere 10 light-years in radius centered on the Sun, we find roughly ten stars. Within a sphere 100 light-years in radius, there are roughly 10,000, or 104, stars, far too many to count or name. Within a 1000-light-year sphere, we find some ten million, or 107. Our galaxy looks like a giant disk with a small ball in the middle. The disk has a diameter of just under 90,000 light years. If we could move outside our galaxy and look down on the disk of the Milky Way from above, it would probably resemble the galaxy in Figure \(\PageIndex{4}\), with its spiral structure outlined by the blue light of stars.

The Sun is a little less than 30,000 light-years from the center of the Milky Way. From our position inside the Milky Way Galaxy, we cannot see through to its far rim because the space between the stars is not completely empty. It contains a sparse distribution of gas, mostly the simplest element, hydrogen, intermixed with tiny solid particles that we call interstellar dust. This gas and dust collect into enormous clouds in many places in the galaxy, becoming the raw material for future generations of stars. Figure \(\PageIndex{5}\) is an image of the disk of the galaxy as seen from our vantage point.

The dust in space, building up over thousands of light-years, can block the light of more distant stars. Luckily, astronomers have found that stars and raw material shine with various forms of light, some of which do penetrate the dust, and so we have been able to develop a pretty good map of the galaxy.

From various investigations, we have evidence that much of our galaxy is made of material we cannot currently observe directly with our instruments. We therefore call this component of the galaxy dark matter. We know the dark matter is there by the pull its gravity exerts on the stars and raw material we can observe, but what this dark matter is made of and how much of it exists remain a mystery. Furthermore, this dark matter is not confined to our galaxy.

By the way, not all stars live by themselves, as the Sun does. Many are born in double or triple systems with two, three, or more stars orbiting around each other. Because the stars influence each other in such close systems, multiple stars allow us to measure characteristics that we cannot discern from observing single stars. In a number of places, enough stars have formed together that we recognized them as star clusters, like the one in Figure \(\PageIndex{6}\). Some of the largest of the star clusters that astronomers have cataloged contain hundreds of thousands of stars and take up volumes of space hundreds of light-years across.

Stars do not last forever. Stars produce energy using hydrogen as fuel. Eventually all stars run out of fuel. This news should not cause you to panic, because our Sun still has a fuel supply that will last at least 5 or 6 billion years. Ultimately, the Sun and all stars will die. We now know that many of the atoms in our bodies were once inside stars. These stars exploded at the ends of their lives, recycling their material back into the reservoir of the galaxy. In this sense, all of us are made of recycled star dust.
