Stellar Evolution

Stellar evolution is the process by which a star experiences a sequence of drastic variations during its stellar life. Depending on the initial stellar mass, a star's lifetime ranges from a few million years for the most massive to trillions of years for the least massive, which is considerably longer than the age of the Universe at 13.8 billion years. For now, let's consider what happens to a low-mass star after it reaches the main sequence.

The Main Sequence  

Once a star has reached the main-sequence stage of its life, it derives its energy almost entirely from the conversion of hydrogen to helium via the process of nuclear fusion in its core. Since hydrogen is the most abundant element in stars, this process can maintain the star’s equilibrium for a long time. Thus, all stars remain on the main sequence for most of their lives. Some astronomers like to call the main-sequence phase the star’s prolonged adolescence or adulthood.

The stars on the left-hand edge of the main sequence in the H–R diagram are classified as zero-age main sequence (ZAMS). The term zero-age refers to the moment when a star stops contracting, settles onto the main sequence, and begins to fuse hydrogen in its core. Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\) depicts the zero-age main sequence as a continuous blue line in the H–R diagram that shows where stars of different masses can be found when they begin to fuse hydrogen.

Since only 0.7% of the hydrogen used in fusion reactions is converted into energy, fusion does not change the total mass of the star by a significant amount during this long period. It does, however, change the chemical composition in its core where nuclear reactions occur. Hydrogen is gradually depleted, and helium accumulates at the center of the star. This change of composition changes the luminosity, temperature, size, and interior structure of the star. When a star’s luminosity and temperature begin to change, the point that represents the star on the H–R diagram moves away from the zero-age main sequence.

Calculations show that the temperature and density in the inner region slowly increase as helium accumulates in the center of a star. As the core temperature increases, each proton acquires more energy of motion on average. This means it is more likely to interact with other protons, and the rate of fusion also increases. The rate of fusion in the proton-proton cycle goes up roughly proportional to the temperature to the fourth power. If the rate of fusion goes up, the rate at which energy is being generated also increases, and the luminosity of the star gradually rises. Initially, these changes are small, and stars remain within the main-sequence band on the H–R diagram for most of their lifetimes.

Example: Star Temperature and Rate of Fusion

If a star’s temperature were to double, by what factor would its rate of fusion increase?

Solution

Since the rate of fusion (like temperature) goes up to the fourth power, it would increase by a factor of 24, or 16 times.

Exercise: Star Temperature and Rate of Fusion

If the rate of fusion of a star increased 256 times, by what factor would the temperature increase?

Answer

 

The temperature would increase by a factor of 2560.25 (that is, the 4th root of 256), or 4 times.

Lifetimes on the Main Sequence

The type and life of a new star depends on the initial amount of material present. Astronomers group stars as low-mass, <1.33 MSun, intermediate-mass, 1.33 MSun to 4 MSun, and high-mass >4 MSun. Note that 1 MSun represents the mass of the Sun, so the Sun is a low-mass star. Each of these star groups has different lives and deaths. You might think that a more massive star, having more fuel, would last longer, but it’s not that simple. The lifetime of a star in a particular stage of evolution depends on how much nuclear fuel it has and on how quickly it uses up that fuel. In the case of stars, more massive ones use up their fuel much more quickly than stars of low mass.

The reason massive stars burn through their hydrogen faster is that the rate of fusion depends very strongly on the star’s core temperature. In a star, the weight of the overlying layers creates pressure in the core. Increasing the mass of a star increases the pressure in the core. Higher pressure leads to higher temperature. The higher the temperature in the core, the faster the star races through its storehouse of central hydrogen. Although massive stars have more fuel, they burn it so quickly that their lifetimes are much shorter than those of their low-mass counterparts. The high rate of fusion also produces more energy, making high-mass stars also the most luminous.

The main-sequence lifetimes of stars of different masses are listed in Table \(\PageIndex{1}\). This table shows that the most massive stars spend only a few million years on the main sequence. A star of 1 solar mass remains there for roughly 10 billion years, while a star of about 0.4 solar mass has a main-sequence lifetime of some 200 billion years, which is longer than the current age of the universe. Remember that every star spends most of its total lifetime on the main sequence, fusing hydrogen into helium. That means that the total lifetimes of the stars in Table \(\PageIndex{1}\) are only a little longer than the ages listed for time on the main sequence.

Human beings developed on a planet around a G-type star. This means that the Sun’s stable main-sequence lifetime is so long that it afforded life on Earth plenty of time to evolve. When searching for intelligent life like our own on planets around other stars, it would be a pretty big waste of time to search around O- or B-type stars. These stars remain stable for such a short time that the development of creatures complicated enough to take astronomy courses is very unlikely.

Helium Core

Over time, a low-mass star consumes all of the hydrogen in its core. The core then contains only helium, along with a small percentage of heavier elements the star had to begin with. Think about a car that runs out of gas. Unless you put more gas in the tank, the car will not run. The same is true with stars; no more fuel in the stellar core will end the stellar fusion process. What happens next?

When a star runs out of hydrogen in its core, it can no longer generate energy. The current core temperature is too low for helium to fuse. The long period of stability now ends, gravity again takes over, and the core begins to contract. Once more, the star’s energy is partially supplied by gravitational energy. As the star’s core shrinks, the energy of the inward-falling material is converted to heat. The heat flows outward to where it is a bit cooler. In the process, the heat raises the temperature of a layer of hydrogen that spent the whole long main-sequence time just outside the core. The additional heat produced by the shrinking core makes this layer hot enough for a shell of hydrogen nuclei just outside the core to begin fusing into helium in a process called hydrogen shell burning.

New energy produced by fusion of this hydrogen now pours outward from this shell and begins to heat up layers of the star farther out, causing them to expand. For a star like the Sun, the red giant star is now larger than the orbit of Mars. Meanwhile, the helium core continues to contract, producing more heat right around it. This leads to more fusion in the shell of fresh hydrogen outside the core. In Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\), Illustration (a) includes the internal structure of a star on the main sequence, including the hydrogen core, and the rest of the star labeled Stellar envelope. Fusion can only take place in the core where it is hot enough for the proton-proton reaction. Illustration (b) includes a star with a small helium core with a donut-shaped hydrogen-burning shell around it. The energy for the star is no longer being produced in its core, but in the shell.   This energy also flows out into the upper layer of the star.

Most stars generate more energy each second when they are fusing hydrogen in the shell surrounding the helium core than they did when hydrogen fusion was confined to the central part of the star. More energy means more light, so the star increases in luminosity, or gets brighter. The energy from the shrinking core and hydrogen-burning shells also causes the outer layers of the star begin to expand. The star eventually grows and grows until it reaches enormous proportions. Figure \(\PageIndex{3}\) demonstrates the very large difference in size between the Sun, Delta Boötis and Xi Cygni. Delta Bootis is a giant star with a diameter that is roughly 11 times the diameter of the Sun. Xi Cygni is too large to be included in the image, but the supergiant has a diameter over 200 times larger than that of the Sun.

When you take the lid off a pot of boiling water, the steam can expand and it cools down. In the same way, the expansion of a star’s outer layers causes the temperature at the surface to decrease. As it cools, the star’s overall color becomes redder. So the star becomes simultaneously more luminous and cooler. On the H–R diagram, the star therefore leaves the main-sequence band and moves upward, increasing in luminosity, and to the right, decreasing in surface temperature. Over time, massive stars become red supergiants, and lower-mass stars like the Sun become red giants. Note that red giant stars do not actually look deep red. Their colors are more like orange or orange-red.

Just how different are these red giants and supergiants from a main-sequence star? Table \(\PageIndex{2}\) compares the Sun with the red supergiant Betelgeuse, which is visible above Orion’s belt as the bright red star that marks the hunter’s armpit, as demonstrated in Figure \(\PageIndex{4}\). Relative to the Sun, this supergiant has a much larger radius, a much lower average density, a cooler surface, and a much hotter core.

Red giants can become so large that if we were to replace the Sun with one of them, its outer atmosphere would extend to the orbit of Mars or even beyond. Figure \(\PageIndex{4}\) includes an image of the constellation Orion with Betelgeuse is marked with a yellow “X” near the top left. In the left image, we see Betelgeuse in ultraviolet with the Hubble Space Telescope, in the first direct image ever made of the surface of another star. As shown by the scale at the bottom, Betelgeuse has an extended atmosphere so large that, if it were at the center of the Solar System, it would stretch past the orbit of Jupiter. This is the next stage in the life of a star as it moves from its long period of youth and adulthood to old age. By considering the relative ages of the Sun and Betelgeuse, we can also see that the idea that bigger stars die faster is indeed true here. Betelgeuse is a mere 10 million years old, which is relatively young compared with our Sun’s 4.5 billion years, but it is already nearing its death throes as a red supergiant.

Modeling the Giant Stage

As we discussed earlier, astronomers can construct computer models of stars with different masses and compositions to see how stars change throughout their lives. Figure \(\PageIndex{5}\), which is based on theoretical calculations by University of Illinois astronomer Icko Iben, shows an H–R diagram with several tracks of evolution from the main sequence to the giant stage of model stars with chemical compositions similar to that of the Sun. The solid black lines show the predicted evolution from the main sequence through the red giant or supergiant stage on the H–R diagram. Each track is labeled with the mass of the star with masses from 0.5 to 15 times the mass of our Sun. The tracks have several dots along the track marking different points in the star's evolution along with a number that indicates the time, in years, required for each star to reach that point.

Tracing the track of the Sun, it moves directly up from the main sequence, increasing in luminosity but remaining at the same surface temperature, until it reaches a point marked 7x109 years, or 7 billion years. The track then curves up and to the right, first increasing in luminosity, then decreasing in surface temperature, to a point labeled 10.4 billion years. The track moves a large distance up and a little to the right, indicating a large increase in luminosity and a medium decrease in temperature. This is where the track ends with the Sun as a giant star at 11.4 billion years. Over this track, the Sun becomes roughly 1,000 times brighter and its surface temperature has cooled from 5800 K to a little less than 4000 K.

The most massive star in this diagram has a mass similar to that of Betelgeuse, and so its evolutionary track shows approximately the history of Betelgeuse. The track of a star like Betelgeuse initially increases a little in luminosity, then cools from roughly 20,000 to 4000 K. The end of the track, where Betelgeuse is a supergiant is labeled 1.20x107, or 70 million years. While theorists debate the exact number of years shown in Figure \(\PageIndex{5}\), the main thing to take from the model results is that the more massive the star, the shorter time it takes for each stage in its life.
