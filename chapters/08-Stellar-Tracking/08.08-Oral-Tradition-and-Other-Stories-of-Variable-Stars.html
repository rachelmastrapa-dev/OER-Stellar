Science and Society - Indigenous Oral Tradition and Ancient Egyptian Stories of Variable Stars

This chapter has been all about how astronomers track the motion and visibility of stars over time. As you now know, these changes are extremely subtle, and sometimes take months, years, if not millennia, to demonstrate any noticeable difference in the night sky. Chapter 2 of the text discussed the methods of ancient astronomers to map the skies and their observations of stars' motion each year, along with more modern methods discussed in this chapter. In addition to measuring stars' motion, ancient astronomers also had methods for observing variable stars.

How does a person document changes in a star's brightness if there is no digital telescope to measure how much light is coming in? In this case, it might help to picture yourself living on Earth in a remote location, before the invention of electricity. During this time, there were not many forms of entertainment after dark, and thus led to humans being very in tune with the night sky. Night after night, one could gaze up at the stars and see the exact same patterns over a lifetime. In the case of variable stars, one need only notice how the brightness of one star compares to those around it. Of course, this is simpler said than done. Australian Aboriginals' Measurements of Giant Variable Stars

The ancient Aboriginal people of Australia are well known as master astronomers with a wide range of documented observations. We will discuss more of their findings in later chapters of this text as well, but their documentation of the star Betelgeuse is an important one to mention. The following paragraphs in this section come from the attributed article by Dr. Bradley Schaefer, who specializes in historical astronomy.

The Australian discovery of Betelgeuse as a variable star comes from lore collected a century ago from southern and central Australia for multiple groups, with two basic stories. The first oral tradition is about a hunter, represented by the modern stars of Orion, chasing after some young sisters (represented by the Pleiades), with his 'fire lust' (represented in the star Betelgeuse) waxing and waning, while the older protective sister's left foot (represented by Aldebaran) also fills and empties of the 'fire magic'. The second story is also a morality tale, with a story about a young male initiate (named Waiyungari) covered in red ochre running away into the sky, where he now sits in a canoe on the Milky Way, flanked by two stars representing two women, being prominent in the September evening skies. Waiyungari is definitively identified as Antares. The lore states that when Waiyungari brightens and gets hotter, this increases the sexual desire of the people.

These are clear statements from multiple communities and multiple ethnographers that the three red stars get brighter and fainter, all told through storytelling and art. This basic claim (that the Aboriginal Australians discovered the variability of Betelgeuse) has excited skepticism in some quarters. The unstated root of this skepticism is the view that Aboriginal people were too "primitive" to be able to make a discovery that is more typical of modern Western science, as well as a general frustration from having heard so many implausible claims of discoveries of lost-ancient-wisdom. The human eye is good for comparing nearby stars, even with a need to glance back and forth (as well known to the many amateur vari- able star observers).

Novice observers can detect substantial differences in brightnesses of near 0.3 magnitude, while well-experienced observers can get as good as 0.10 magnitude. These approximate numbers are well known, and have been confirmed in a variety of experiments with many subjects, as well as large-scale experiments with the American Association of Variable Star Observers.

What is needed is a small set of relatively nearby comparison stars, best with their magnitudes at the top, middle, and bottom of the range. And that is exactly what we have for Betelgeuse (and Aldebaran and Antares). But why should Aboriginal Australians (or any other culture outside of the Greek-descended tradition) be looking for variability, much less pass it on? Well, there is no particular reason to expect that the Aboriginal Australians did any systematic variability-searching. But systematic searches are not needed for discovery. For the many waking hours during each night, the only light away from the campfire is up in the stars, so these were frequently seen throughout the lives of Aboriginal people.

Betelgeuse is among the brightest dozen or so stars, while it is in a striking pattern in modern Orion, and has a striking color. So, if you are repeatedly looking at stars, then Betelgeuse would be a natural and frequent target, especially while living under dark skies with no light pollution.

Even today, Betelgeuse continues to drastically change in brightness (the reasoning for which will be discussed more in Chapter 13). Figure \(\PageIndex{1}\) is one example from 2019-2020 in which Betelgeuse changed by about 1.2 magnitude, a period now referred to as the Great Dimming.

Indigenous people the world over are renown for exhaustive observations of all of nature around them, as it is critical for survival. So it is inevitable that many Aboriginal people over the millennia would watch Betelgeuse sufficiently closely to easily recognize that it varies from brighter-than to much-fainter-than Procyon. No written records are needed for such observations, and the memory of prior brightness extremes needs to be kept for only half a year or so.2 Thus, it is reasonable and inevitable that many Aboriginal people (and many people from other cultures) over the millennia discovered the variability of Betelgeuse

Egyptian Measurements of Algol

As with many ancient civilizations, astronomical observations had major influence on everyday life for ancient Egyptians. Scribes wrote Calendars of Lucky and Unlucky Days, which assigned good or bad prognoses for each day of the year. The Cairo Calendar, dated 1271-1163 BCE, was a particularly influential record of events, and is one of the best preserved for studying such astronomical observations.

Recent studies of the Cairo Calendar (CC) have shown that Egyptian Scribes documented the period of an eclipsing binary star, called Algol. The next chapter will describe eclipsing binary stars in more depth. For now, the main idea is that two closely orbiting stars, which cannot be resolved as individual points from such a vast distance, will appear to change brightness as one star passes in front of the other from our point-of-view on Earth. Figure \(\PageIndex{2}\) is an artist's impression of an eclipsing binary star system, though the Algol system contains two stars of different size and temperature.

Ancient Egyptians noticed this phenomenon in the star system called Algol, and appear to have measured this change in brightness as the two stars eclipse one another. This is a significant realization, as these ancient observers were not using telescopes, but just their naked eyes.

While the Egyptians did not name this star system specifically in the CC, multiple studies have shown this star is the only logical choice in which the calendar describes such a specific period of time and associates it with changing brightness. Modern astronomers have measured the star system to have an eclipse period of 2.867 days, which closely matches the Egyptian prognosis of luck, with a period of 2.850 days.

Along with several other nightly measurements of the stars, tracking the period of variability became another record for time keeping. There is some discrepancy on how the Egyptians interpreted the changing brightness of this star system, and whether it was associated with any specific mythological symbols of the time. However, the name Algol is derived from the Arabic word, head of the Ghoul (ra's al-gh¯ul), and appears many times in historical texts up to medieval times with a variety of associations of violence and bad luck.
