Locating Places in the Sky

Celestial cartography, also known as uranography or star cartography, is the area of astronomy that produces star maps. Most are aware of cartographers who made early and more recent maps of Earth. Both types of cartography were difficult in early history; we were not certain of the exact sizes and shapes of land masses, and there are many, many stars to chart correctly. Many early Earth and star maps were very artistic in nature and truly took not only a scientific mind, but the skills of an amazing artist.

Celestial objects are shown on planispheres, star charts and maps, and star atlases, as well as computer and tablet applications and programs. The printed planispheres and charts use different size “dots” to represent the object’s brightness. Many of the new star chart computer and tablet applications and programs are able to show star brightness without using a larger or smaller dot.

The stars on modern charts are mapped using right ascension and declination. Right ascension is simlar to longitude, and is measured in degrees or hours, while declination is like latitude and is measured in positive degrees above the celestial equator and negative degrees below.

The charts, maps, and applications show the object’s apparent magnitude. The apparent magnitude scale is based on what we see, and does not truly compare a star’s true brightness to other stars. So astronomers devised a second scale: Absolute Magnitude, M. This scale defines brightness as if we moved all the stars to the same distance about 30 light years. These measurements, albeit hypothetical since we cannot move objects, are the measure of a celestial object’s intrinsic brightness. This allows astronomers and astrophysicists to directly compare star brightness’; comparing apples to apples .

Take our Sun, for example. It is so close, relative to the other stars and celestial objects, that it appears to be very bright, with an apparent magnitude -26. 74 Mv. Yet move the Sun to the hypothetical distance of 30 light years, and the Sun becomes a relatively faint 4. 83 M absolute magnitude object.
