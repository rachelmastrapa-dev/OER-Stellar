Figure 6.1.1 Earth and the Sun

An image comparing the size of Earth to a portion of the Sun's surface and a giant loop of erupting gas.

Image Elements

  • A close-up view of the solar surface shows a large loop of hot, erupted gas arcing above it.
  • Next to the loop, a small dot is labeled Earth for size comparison, appearing tiny against the loop and the Sun's surface.
  • An inset at the bottom right shows the entire Sun, much smaller in the frame.

Credit: SOHO/EIT/ESA

Figure 6.1.2 Parts of the Sun

A cutaway diagram of the Sun labeling its interior layers and surface features.

Diagram Elements

  • A triangular wedge is removed from the upper half of the Sun to expose the interior, while surface features are shown in the lower half.
  • Interior features are labeled on the left side: the core, drawn in blue at the center; the radiative zone, drawn as a gradient from yellow near the core to orange and red at its outer edge, with wavy arrows showing energy moving outward; and the convection zone, a thick yellow layer above it with oval arrows showing the vertical motion of rising and sinking gas.
  • Atmosphere and surface features are labeled on the left and right sides: photosphere, chromosphere, corona, a sunspot, a prominence, and a coronal hole.

Credit: NASA/Goddard

Figure 6.1.5 The Sun's Atmosphere

A composite image combining three views of the Sun to show its three atmospheric layers.

Image Elements

  • The four sections combine to form a single circular image of the Sun.
  • The upper left quarter shows the Sun in ordinary visible light, with the photosphere and a sunspot labeled.
  • The upper right quarter shows the Sun in H-alpha light, with the chromosphere labeled.
  • The bottom half shows the Sun in X-rays, with the corona, a coronal hole, coronal loops, and an active region labeled.

Credit: NASA

Figure 6.1.6 Solar Atmosphere Temperatures

A graph of temperature versus height above the photosphere in the solar atmosphere.

Chart Elements

  • The horizontal axis is labeled Height in Solar Atmosphere (km) and ranges from 0 to 3000.
  • The vertical axis is labeled Temperature (K) and ranges from 103 to 106.
  • The line begins at 0 km near 104 K, and drops to about 3 x 103 K by 500 km, in the region labeled Photosphere.
  • It then rises to about 6 x 103 K by 1000 km and stays near that temperature to 2000 km, in the region labeled Chromosphere.
  • From 2000 km onward, the line rises steeply through the region labeled Transition region, around 1.5 x 105 K, and levels off in the region labeled Corona, above 106 K.

Figure 6.2.4 Magnetogram and Solar Cycle

A magnetogram of sunspot polarity beside a sequence of images tracking the solar magnetic cycle over several years.

Image (a): Magnetogram

  • On the left
  • A magnetogram of the Sun shows the magnetic polarity of sunspots.
  • Black areas mark magnetism pointing toward the Sun's core.
  • White areas mark magnetism pointing away from the core, toward the viewer.

Image (b): Solar Cycle Sequence

  • On the right
  • Ten maps of the Sun's surface magnetic field are arranged in an arc, spanning a period of 7.5 years.
  • The two magnetic polarities, north and south, are shown against a blue disk: dark blue to black for north, light blue to white for south.
  • The earliest map, from January 8, 1992, is at the lower left, taken just after solar maximum; each following map, moving along the arc, was taken roughly half a year to a year later.
  • The last map, from July 25, 1999, was taken as the Sun approached the next solar maximum.
  • Across the maps, the direction of the pattern from white to black polarity in the Southern Hemisphere is opposite that in the Northern Hemisphere.

Credit (a): NASA/SDO; Credit (b): NASA/SOHO

Figure 6.2.5 Twisted Magnetic Field Lines

Three diagrams showing how the Sun's differential rotation winds its magnetic field lines into loops over time.

Diagram Elements

  • Three illustrations of the Sun are shown in sequence from left to right, representing increasing time.
  • In the leftmost diagram, magnetic field lines stretch from the bottom to the top of the Sun, with arrows indicating differential rotation.
  • In the middle diagram, the field lines are pulled to the right at the Sun's equator, beginning to wind up.
  • In the rightmost diagram, the wound-up field lines have morphed into loops.

Figure 6.3.2 Numbers of Sunspots over Time

A graph titled Monthly Average Sunspot Numbers, showing sunspot counts from 1750 to 2000.

  • The horizontal axis shows the year, ranging from 1750 to 2000.

Figure 6.5.2 Large Datasets

A graph showing the growth in the number of objects cataloged in astronomical surveys over time.

Chart Elements

  • The vertical axis shows the number of cataloged astronomical objects on a logarithmic scale, and the horizontal axis shows the year, beginning around 1924.
  • Early catalogs, such as the Henry Draper Catalog of about 20,000 stars, appear near the bottom left.
  • Later surveys climb steadily higher: the Sloan Digital Sky Survey catalogs roughly 200 million objects, and the Dark Energy Survey catalogs nearly 1 billion objects, more than four times as many.
  • A projected point for the Large Synoptic Survey Telescope appears at the upper right, showing an expected catalog of billions of objects, dwarfing all earlier surveys.
